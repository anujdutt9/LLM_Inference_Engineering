10-K Comparison: NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, Broadcom

Scope and sources. Comparison of each company's latest annual report (Form 10-K), based on the excerpts in data/filings/: NVIDIA fiscal 2026 (year ended January 25, 2026), AMD fiscal 2025 (calendar year 2025), Intel fiscal 2025 (calendar year 2025), and Broadcom fiscal 2025 (year ended November 2, 2025) [avgo-item7-p001]. Because the fiscal calendars differ, each company's year-over-year comparison is on its own fiscal calendar. Every claim below is cited to its source anchor in the filings.

Summary

The four filings describe four very different positions in the AI infrastructure cycle. NVIDIA is the dominant supplier of AI data-center infrastructure — it calls itself "a data center scale AI infrastructure company" [nvda-item1-p001] — and posted $215.9 billion of revenue, up 65%, with a 71.1% gross margin and 60.4% operating margin [nvda-item7-p004][nvda-item7-p009]. AMD is the fastest-growing challenger: $34.6 billion of revenue, up 34%, driven by EPYC processors and Instinct GPUs, and it has locked in a 6-gigawatt GPU purchase agreement with OpenAI [amd-item7-p002][amd-item7-p005]. Broadcom is the high-margin, capital-returning company: $63.9 billion of revenue, up 23.9%, with a 67.8% gross margin, selling custom AI accelerators (XPUs) and data-center networking silicon to hyperscalers [avgo-item8-income-p001][avgo-item7-p001]. Intel is the outlier — the only company with declining revenue ($52.9 billion, down 0.5%) and an operating loss of $2.2 billion — having written down its Gaudi AI accelerator effort as "unsuccessful" and shifted its AI bet to GPUs and its 14A foundry node [intc-item8-income-p001][intc-item1a-p004].

  • Customer concentration:
    "A significant amount of our revenue stems from a limited number of partners and distributors and we have a concentration of sales to customers who purchase directly or indirectly from us" [nvda-item1a-p002]
  • Export restrictions:
    "We are subject to complex laws, rules, regulations, and political and other actions, including restrictions on the export of our products, which may adversely impact our business" [nvda-item1a-p002]
  • Dependency on third-party suppliers for manufacturing, assembly, test and packaging [nvda-item1a-p001].

Red flags (financial)

  • $4.5 billion charge in Q1 FY26 for H20 excess inventory and purchase obligations after China export licensing requirements [nvda-item7-p002].
  • Gross margin down 3.9 points to 71.1%, driven by the transition from Hopper HGX systems to Blackwell datacenter solutions plus the H20 charge [nvda-item7-p003][nvda-item7-p004].
  • Inventory provisions had an unfavorable gross-margin impact of 2.6% in FY26 (2.3% in FY25) [nvda-item7-p007].
  • $17.5 billion invested in private companies and infrastructure funds, "many of these investments are illiquid and non-marketable" [nvda-item7-p006].

How their positions differ in AI and data-center demand

  • NVIDIA — the full-stack leader.
    "NVIDIA is now a data center scale AI infrastructure company reshaping all industries" [nvda-item1-p001]
    Data Center revenue grew 68% in FY26 [nvda-item7-p005]; within it, Data Center computing grew 59% on the Blackwell platform and Data Center networking grew 142% on NVLink compute fabric for GB200/GB300 systems [nvda-item7-p011]. Blackwell's "extreme co-design" spans chips, networking, systems and software:
    "Hundreds of thousands of GPUs can be interconnected to function as a single giant computer" [nvda-item1-p001]
  • AMD — the GPU challenger. Its Data Center segment grew 32% to $16.6 billion on EPYC processors and Instinct MI350 GPUs [amd-item7-p002]. The OpenAI deal puts AMD's MI450 in the first gigawatt of a 6-gigawatt deployment [amd-item7-p005], and the ZT Systems acquisition (retaining the design business) was aimed at building complex AI solutions [amd-item7-p004]. Export controls have already bitten: ~$440M of charges on the MI308 [amd-item7-p002].
  • Intel — the foundry bet after the accelerator stumble. Intel concedes it "missed the significant shift in compute demand to GPUs" and that its Gaudi accelerator effort "was unsuccessful" [intc-item1a-p004]. Its AI path now runs through a partnership with NVIDIA to "co-develop custom client and data center products combining our x86 CPU technologies with NVIDIA's AI and accelerated computing capabilities" [intc-item7-p007], while its strategic bet is the 18A/14A foundry — with 14A explicitly at risk of being paused without a significant external customer [intc-item7-p002][intc-item7-p009].
  • Broadcom — the custom-silicon alternative. Broadcom sells "custom accelerators or XPUs" to hyperscalers, plus "Ethernet switching and routing silicon, Ethernet NICs, physical layer devices (PHYs) and optical components" for large-scale AI data centers [avgo-item7-p001]. Unlike NVIDIA's and AMD's general-purpose GPUs, Broadcom's AI silicon is "built to our individual customers' specifications" [avgo-item7-p001] — a differentiated position in the AI data-center supply chain.
